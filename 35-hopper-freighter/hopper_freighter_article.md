# The Hopper Freighter

### Every settlement eventually discovers that its roads end long before its ambitions do.

Surface transport is bounded by terrain in a way that's easy to underestimate.

A rover on the Moon or Mars must negotiate craters, boulder fields, steep slopes, soft dust,
rilles and canyons and scarps — and it must do all of it *slowly*, because the terrain isn't
mapped in fine detail and a stuck or overturned vehicle is unrecoverable. Real rovers have
travelled at walking pace or slower and covered tens of kilometres over *years*.

Paving helps enormously, and the sintered roads of the previous concept make the busiest routes
fast and clean. But paving is slow and power-hungry, and it can only ever cover a tiny fraction
of a world.

Meanwhile a settlement will need to reach places far outside its paved network: an ice deposit
at a distant pole, a lava tube skylight spotted from orbit, a mineral outcrop, a stranded asset,
a second base. Some lie thousands of kilometres away across terrain no wheeled vehicle can cross
in any reasonable time.

For those journeys the answer isn't a better rover. It's a different mode altogether — going
*over* the ground instead of across it.

## Arc over it

The HF-1 "Grasshopper" is an uncrewed cargo lander built for repeated point-to-point flights on
a planetary surface.

It stands on a prepared pad, loaded with cargo, and lifts off vertically on rocket thrust. It
doesn't accelerate to orbital speed — it burns just long enough to place itself on a **ballistic
arc**, a suborbital trajectory that rises, coasts over the intervening terrain, and falls toward
the destination. Near the target it flips, burns again to cancel its descent, and settles onto
landing legs. Cargo out, refuel, next leg.

Ground that would take a rover months to cross — if it could cross it at all — takes under an
hour.

Everything about the design is simplified by the absence of air. No wings, no streamlining, no
heat shield, because there's nothing to fly through and nothing to heat the vehicle on arrival.
The hopper can be a bare frame of tanks, engines, legs, and cargo mounts. The trajectory is pure
ballistics — calculable in advance and undisturbed by weather or wind, because neither exists.

## Why hopping is cheap here and not on Earth

A ballistic hop follows the physics of a thrown stone: speed and angle plus gravity determine
where you land. The energy needed grows with distance and with the strength of gravity — and it
gets spent **twice**, once to launch and once to arrest the descent, because without an
atmosphere there's no free braking. That second burn is the essential difference from Earth,
where a returning vehicle sheds most of its speed against the air.

The Moon is unusually favourable. Gravity is a sixth of Earth's, so the speed for a given range
is much lower and propellant demand falls accordingly. No atmosphere means no drag losses and no
aerodynamic constraints. Long-range hops become achievable at speeds well below what it takes to
reach lunar orbit.

Mars is harder — stronger gravity, and a thin atmosphere that's enough to require care but not
enough to provide useful braking for a compact vehicle. Still far easier than the equivalent on
Earth.

The critical enabling factor on either world is **locally produced propellant**. Hopping is
thirsty, and it only becomes routine when propellant is made on site from local water rather than
imported — precisely the capability the harvester and refinery concepts in this catalogue provide.

## Its niche, defined by the alternatives

The hopper's place is sharply bounded, and that's what makes it clear.

For routine freight between fixed nearby points, **rail on a sintered bed** is far cheaper,
because it costs electricity rather than propellant. For bulk export off the world entirely, the
**mass driver** is cheaper still. The hopper is the vehicle for what neither can do: reaching an
arbitrary point on the surface, quickly, with no infrastructure waiting at the destination.

It's the settlement's aircraft — expensive per tonne-kilometre, indispensable for reach. Its
natural cargoes are high-value, urgent, or exploratory: survey gear to a remote site, spares to a
stranded outpost, the first delivery to a place where no base exists and no road will ever go.

**Roads carry the tonnage. The hopper carries the reach.**

## The honest difficulties

Propellant governs everything. Every hop pays twice, with no atmosphere to brake against, which
makes hopping expensive per tonne and ties the concept entirely to a working local propellant
industry. Without in-situ production, a hopper fleet is simply unaffordable.

Landing is the second difficulty and the more dangerous one. The vehicle must arrive precisely
and settle safely, possibly on unprepared ground, entirely autonomously — and a rocket landing on
loose regolith blasts debris outward at high speed, which both threatens the vehicle and argues
for sintered pads at every regularly used destination. Reliability is critical because there's no
rescue: an engine that fails to restart mid-flight loses the vehicle and its cargo.

And the whole approach is wasteful for any journey a road could serve.

None of this is a barrier of physics — suborbital hopping is elementary rocketry and lunar
landings have been proven since Apollo. This is a vehicle waiting on a *fuel supply*, not on an
invention.

## Why it matters

On a world where building a road means fusing the ground one slow strip at a time, the discovery
that your roads end early comes early.

The Hopper Freighter answers it by refusing to deal with the ground at all — rising on a column of
thrust, arcing over craters and rille and rubble alike, and setting down an hour later a thousand
kilometres away at a place no wheel will reach this century.

It's thirsty. It will always be the expensive way to move a tonne. It can't fly until the
settlement learns to make its own fuel. But it's the difference between a civilisation confined to
its paved paths and one for which the whole world is within a morning's flight.
