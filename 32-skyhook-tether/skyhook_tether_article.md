# The Skyhook Tether

### The orbital sling that makes the solar system smaller — one silent, fuel-less throw at a time.

Getting off a world's surface is the hardest, most celebrated part of spaceflight. But it
isn't the whole journey.

A payload that reaches a low orbit is still deep in a gravity well relative to where it often
needs to be — a higher orbit, an escape trajectory, a transfer to another world. Each of those
steps needs a further increase in speed, and each is normally paid for with propellant hauled
up at great cost from below. The mass driver of the previous concept, and rockets generally,
can put cargo into low orbit. This concept answers the next question: how do you lift it from
there without burning yet more fuel?

The answer is to borrow the momentum from something that already has it in abundance — a large
object already in orbit.

## A sling that trades momentum

The Skyhook Tether is a long cable in orbit with a massive station or counterweight at its
centre, set spinning end over end as it orbits.

The rotation is arranged so that at the lowest point of its swing, one end of the tether dips
down and momentarily moves *backward* relative to its orbital motion — that is, more slowly over
the ground — reaching low and slow enough to meet a payload climbing up from below. At that
instant the payload is caught and attached to the tether tip. The tether carries it around and
up, and at the top of the swing, half a rotation later, the tip is moving *forward and fast* —
and there the payload is released, flung away at a much greater speed and altitude than it
arrived with.

The elegance is in the timing of the two moments. The catch happens where the tip is moving
slowly relative to the incoming payload, so the two don't have to match the full orbital speed
difference — the tether's rotation cancels most of it. The release happens where the tip is
moving fast, handing that speed to the payload *for free*. A single well-designed rotating
tether can take cargo from a low orbit and throw it onto a transfer toward the Moon, another
planet, or a higher orbit — entirely by mechanical exchange of momentum, with no propellant
burned in the throw.

## The momentum has to balance

Here's the bookkeeping, and it's the heart of the concept.

Momentum is conserved. Each time the tether flings a payload, the payload *gains* momentum and
the tether *loses* an equal amount — which slows the tether and drops its orbit slightly. If
nothing replaced that, the tether would eventually spiral down.

But the momentum can be restored *without propellant*, in two ways. Electrically: a conducting
tether can push against the planet's magnetic field to raise its orbit over time. Or by
symmetry: catch payloads coming the *other* way — descending traffic that gives momentum back
to the tether as it's lowered gently toward the surface. A tether that balances upward and
downward traffic, or slowly recharges electrically, can run indefinitely as a genuinely
propellant-free lift. It's a closed momentum economy, not a fuel tank.

## Better together: the launcher and the sling

The skyhook is the natural partner to the mass driver.

A launcher on a moon throws rugged cargo up to a low altitude but can't easily give it the
further boost to a transfer orbit. A skyhook waiting above can catch that cargo at the top of
its arc and fling it onward. Together they form a **propellant-free chain**: electricity
accelerates cargo off the surface, and orbital momentum lifts it the rest of the way, with fuel
spent nowhere in the sequence.

And it works for worlds with atmospheres too — catching payloads brought up by a suborbital
rocket or spaceplane to a modest height and speed, and completing their climb to orbit. That
halves the velocity a launch vehicle has to supply, which transforms its economics.

## The honest catch: the catch

The defining difficulty is exactly what it sounds like — the catch.

Capturing a payload at the tether's tip demands that the payload and the tip meet at exactly the
right place, time, and relative speed, with the tether rotating *and* orbiting and the payload
climbing to meet it. It's a rendezvous of unforgiving precision, and a failed catch is a serious
event. The tether itself has to be strong enough to swing a payload without breaking, which for
large payloads demands very strong materials and clever multi-strand designs that resist both
tension and small impacts over a long life. And managing the tether's momentum so it neither
spirals down nor drifts up takes an effective restoration system and careful traffic scheduling.

None of it is a barrier of physics — momentum-exchange tethers are a thoroughly analysed idea,
and space tethers of various kinds have already flown. But the precision of the capture and the
strength of the tether are real engineering hurdles, and honesty puts the catch first.

## Why it matters

A rocket climbs by throwing part of itself away. A skyhook climbs by *borrowing* speed and
giving it back later — so nothing is thrown away at all.

It reaches down into a low orbit, catches a rising payload at a gentle relative speed, and slings
it upward on the strength of its own orbital motion, paying the momentum forward and recovering
it from returning traffic or the planet's magnetic field. The catch is delicate and the tether
must be strong, and it's worth building only as part of a chain. But in that chain, paired with a
launcher below, it turns the punishing second half of the climb into a free ride on momentum that
was already there.
