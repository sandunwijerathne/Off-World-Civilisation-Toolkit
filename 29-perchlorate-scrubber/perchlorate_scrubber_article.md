# The Perchlorate Scrubber

### Mars offers ground that is at once a threat and a gift: a dust that would poison you, built around an ion that is four-fifths oxygen.

One of the more sobering discoveries of Mars exploration was that the soil isn't just
infertile — it's actively toxic.

Measurements at the Martian surface found perchlorate salts spread through the regolith at
about half a percent by mass. That sounds small, but it's far above the level at which
perchlorate is a health concern. Perchlorates interfere with the thyroid's uptake of iodine,
are toxic in quantity, and damage many plants. A dust this laden with them is a material to
be *feared*, not farmed.

And on Mars, the dust is everywhere: fine, wind-borne, electrostatically clinging, tracked
into every airlock and settling on every surface.

That poses a double problem. The dream of growing food directly in Martian soil runs
immediately into the fact that the soil would poison the crops and, through them, the crew.
And even setting farming aside, a toxic dust that infiltrates habitats and coats everything
is a chronic health hazard the crew can't escape. Making Martian regolith safe — to grow in,
to live among — isn't an optional refinement. It's a prerequisite for using the planet's own
ground at all.

## Wash it out, then destroy it

The PS-1 "Hygeia" takes in raw, perchlorate-laden regolith and returns clean, detoxified
soil. It turns on one convenient chemical fact: perchlorate salts are highly *soluble* in
water.

So the first step is to wash the regolith — mix it with water, and the perchlorate dissolves
out of the soil and into the water, leaving the mineral grains behind. Separate the washed
soil from the liquid, and you have soil that's safe to use. The perchlorate has just been
moved from the solid into a contaminated brine.

Then you destroy the perchlorate in that brine rather than merely relocating it — and here
the concept has several routes. The most elegant is biological: certain microbes use
perchlorate the way we use oxygen, consuming it and reducing it to harmless chloride and —
significantly — **oxygen**. A tank of these microbes digests the brine, leaving clean reusable
water and releasing oxygen gas. Or you break the perchlorate down with heat or chemistry.
Whichever route, the cycle closes: the water is recovered and reused to wash the next batch,
the soil comes out clean, and the destroyed perchlorate gives up its oxygen.

## A poison made of oxygen

Here's the thing that makes this concept more than a decontaminator.

The perchlorate ion is a single chlorine atom bound to *four* oxygen atoms. That structure is
the key to everything. It's what makes perchlorate a strong oxidiser and a thyroid disruptor —
the toxicity you have to remove. But it's also what makes it a remarkable *store* of oxygen:
four oxygen atoms per ion, so breaking the ion apart liberates a great deal of it.

The very feature that makes the salt dangerous makes it valuable.

So the scrubber is better understood as a resource extractor than a decontaminator. Destroying
the perchlorate doesn't just neutralise a poison — it releases breathable oxygen, from a source
that blankets the entire planet. The biological route is especially appealing because the
microbes work at modest temperatures and yield oxygen directly; it has the same character as
the algal loop elsewhere in this catalogue, a living system harnessed to a chemical task. A
settlement that must scrub its soil anyway can, in the same process, harvest oxygen from the
ground — turning Mars's most notorious hazard into one of its assets.

## The honest catch: containment

The chemistry here is easy and even generous. The hard part is *handling*.

The scrubber exists to process a toxic dust, and doing that at scale, continuously, without
exposing the crew, is genuinely difficult on a planet where the dust already contaminates every
surface and infiltrates every seal. The intake of dry perchlorate-rich regolith, the handling
of concentrated brine, and the disposal of residues all demand rigorous sealed processing. The
wet process helps a lot — wetting the dust immediately suppresses it — but the containment burden
is real and central.

Water is a genuine cost too; the wash consumes it, and on Mars water is precious, so it has to be
recovered efficiently, tying the scrubber to the base's water loop. The biological route, elegant
as it is, means keeping a microbial culture healthy and contained, with the fragility of any
living system. And detoxified soil isn't automatically *fertile* — removing the poison makes it
safe, but making it grow crops well is a further step of adding nutrients and structure.

None of it is a barrier of chemistry; perchlorate reduction is well understood. These are the
practical costs of cleaning a poisoned world.

## Why it matters

Mars hands its would-be settlers a paradox: ground that would poison a crew and their crops,
built around an ion that is four-fifths oxygen.

The Perchlorate Scrubber takes that paradox by both horns — washing the poison from the soil and
then destroying it in a way that hands back breathable oxygen, turning the planet's most feared
material into safe earth and free air in a single process. In return it asks for the discipline to
handle a toxic dust without being harmed by it, which is no small thing on a world made of the
stuff.

But a civilisation cannot farm ground it dare not touch, and the first step to making Mars a home
is to make its soil safe to hold. This is the machine that does it — and, in doing it, breathes.
