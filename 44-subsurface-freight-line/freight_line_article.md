# The Subsurface Freight Line

### The lava had no idea where we'd want to go — but it dug the tunnels anyway.

In terrestrial rail engineering, tunnelling dominates the budget. Boring through rock is slow,
expensive, and dangerous, and the cost per kilometre dwarfs open track by a wide margin. It's why
railways go *around* mountains where they can and through them only when they must.

An off-world settlement faces every one of those costs plus several Earth doesn't, because it has
neither the machinery, the workforce, nor the energy budget for large-scale excavation.

And yet both the Moon and Mars appear to be threaded with tunnels that were excavated for free, by
geology, billions of years ago.

Lava tubes form where a river of molten rock crusts over while the liquid beneath drains away,
leaving a hollow passage under a solid roof. Orbital observations and GRAIL gravity data support
the existence of large empty tubes beneath the lunar maria — and in lunar gravity they could be far
bigger than anything on Earth.

If even a fraction of one is straight enough, level enough, and stable enough to carry track, a
settlement gets a sheltered freight corridor for the cost of *surveying and clearing* rather than
digging. That's a completely different economic proposition, and it's the whole argument for this
concept.

## A deliberately austere railway

The SF-1 "Cuniculus" is a fixed-route freight line laid along a tube floor between two points a
settlement connects repeatedly — a mine and a refinery, a refinery and a launcher, a habitat
cluster and its power plant.

Rail beats road here for the same reason it does on Earth: for heavy, repetitive traffic between
fixed points, a guided vehicle on a prepared way is more efficient, more easily automated, and
gentler on its running surface than something free-roaming.

The line is austere on purpose. **The tube isn't pressurised.** Cargo travels in sealed containers
on unpressurised flatbeds, because pressurising kilometres of natural cavern with an irregular,
possibly cracked roof is an enormous undertaking for a benefit — shirt-sleeve maintenance — that
doesn't come close to justifying it. Power and control run along the track. Access is through the
same skylights the surveyor descended, fitted with hoists, and stations sit beneath those openings
where surface traffic meets underground traffic.

## The real problem isn't the tunnel. It's the wheel.

Here's the bit that's easy to state and easy to forget.

A vehicle's **mass** measures how hard it is to accelerate, and it doesn't change with location.
Its **weight** is the force gravity exerts on it — and on the Moon that's about a sixth of the
terrestrial value.

Traction at a wheel depends on *weight*. The maximum force a wheel can transmit before slipping is
set by how hard it's pressed against the rail.

So: a loaded wagon on the Moon needs **exactly the same force to accelerate** as it would on Earth,
while the grip available to supply that force has dropped **sixfold**.

Inertia stays. Grip vanishes.

The same asymmetry applies in reverse to braking, which is the more alarming half — you can't stop
using friction you don't have.

Conventional adhesion railways are therefore badly suited to low gravity. They'd spin under any
useful acceleration and struggle to stop. The fix is to stop relying on friction at all: drive the
train with **linear motors**, which react electromagnetically against a reaction rail rather than
gripping it. The wheels then just support and guide the vehicle instead of propelling it. It's the
same principle as the lunar mass driver, run continuously and far more gently — and it makes
braking regenerative and equally grip-independent.

Against that difficulty, the environment offers a compensating gift. With no atmosphere there's no
aerodynamic drag, and steel-on-steel rolling resistance is already tiny. Once a train is moving,
keeping it moving costs almost nothing. The energy bill is essentially all starting and stopping.

## What the roof buys — and what it refuses to give

The overburden is the reason to be down there, and its benefits are substantial and mostly free:

- **Radiation** — tens of metres of rock is a formidable shield against cosmic rays and solar
  particle events
- **Micrometeorites** — stopped outright
- **Thermal cycling** — this one's underrated. A surface railway on the Moon would expand and
  contract through an enormous swing every lunar day, and cycling that severe reliably destroys
  rails, joints, bearings, and cabling. Underground it's near-constant, so track can be laid
  without elaborate expansion provisions.
- **Dust** — on Mars, seasonal storms simply excluded

What the tube *won't* give you is **alignment**.

A lava tube follows the path the lava took, which owes nothing to where you want your freight to
go. It meanders, changes gradient, narrows, and is strewn with breakdown blocks fallen from the
roof — the very rubble that proves the roof spalls. A railway needs gentle curves and gentle
gradients, so the floor has to be cleared and graded, tight sections bypassed or accepted as speed
restrictions, unstable stretches supported or avoided.

The honest summary: **the tube supplies the expensive part of a tunnel — the hole — and none of the
cheap parts, which are the ones that turn a hole into a railway.**

## The honest difficulties

**The route isn't yours to choose**, and that's the governing constraint. The odds that a given
settlement has a suitably straight, level, stable tube running between two places it actually needs
to connect are uncertain and site-specific. The concept may simply not apply at many locations.

**Stability is the sharpest worry.** Breakdown blocks on the floor are direct evidence that roofs
shed themselves. Putting irreplaceable infrastructure under a ceiling with that track record demands
survey, monitoring, and in places active support.

**Traction must be solved electromagnetically**, which means a powered track rather than a passive
one — far more installed hardware per kilometre.

**Communications don't propagate out of a cave**, so routine maintenance inherits the surveyor's
isolation problems.

And it's only worth building where the *same heavy cargo moves between the same two points
repeatedly*. For anything else, a rover or a hopper wins.

## Why it matters

Every component of this concept exists except the site. Linear motors are in daily service on
Earth. Rail engineering needs no defence. The tubes are supported by gravity data, and their
shielding value is characterised in the literature.

What's missing is knowledge of the *interior* — whether any particular tube is straight enough,
level enough, and sound enough to carry a line. And that's precisely what the lava tube surveyor
exists to return.

This concept is downstream of that one in the strictest sense: it can't be designed, only imagined,
until a surveyor has gone down and come back with a map. The near-term step isn't track. It's
reconnaissance.

Given a tube that runs the right way, this is the cheapest heavy freight a settlement will ever
have. The difficulty is that the geology had no idea where we'd want to go.
