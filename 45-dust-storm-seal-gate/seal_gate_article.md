# The Dust-Storm Seal Gate

### The hull will hold. The door is where the planet gets in.

An airlock is usually described as a pressure device — two doors and a volume between them, so a
habitat can be entered without being emptied.

On a dusty world it's something else as well, and the something else is much harder.

Every person, tool, wheel, and container passing through carries the planet's surface with it. And
lunar and Martian dust is fine, clinging, abrasive, and — on Mars — chemically hostile. The pressure
problem is solved. The dust problem is not.

This isn't hypothetical. Apollo crews spent *days* on the lunar surface and still found dust among
their most serious practical problems: it clung to suits, got tracked into the cabin, degraded
seals, abraded fabric and visors, and resisted every attempt to remove it. A settlement planning to
stay for decades, cycling airlocks many times a day, is exposed to that same process continuously.

You can make a hull very good. Buried, sintered, self-healing, all three. Then someone opens a door,
and the boundary is only as good as the procedure at that door.

## A graded transition, not a chamber

The SG-1 "Janus" is a sequence of zones, each dirtier than the next, with dust never permitted to
travel backwards.

**Zone 1 — gross removal.** Mechanical agitation, brushing of large debris, and on Mars a gas jet,
since there's at least *some* atmosphere to blow with. Sheds the coarse fraction quickly.

**Zone 2 — the fine removal that actually matters.** Electrodynamic sweeping across floor, wall, and
fixture surfaces, plus magnetic capture.

**Zone 3 — the pressure lock.** The only part a terrestrial engineer would recognise as an airlock.

Two commitments follow from taking dust seriously. The gate is **directional**: airflow, drainage,
and surface slopes all run outward, so everything dislodged migrates back toward the planet rather
than deeper into the settlement. And the gate is a **designated dirty facility** with its own
maintenance regime — understood to be permanently contaminated and built accordingly, with surfaces
that can be swept electrically and components that expect abrasive grit.

The name fits. It faces two ways at once, and has to be honest about which face is which.

## Why dust sticks, and how to make it leave

Fine dust adheres far more stubbornly than intuition suggests, for several reasons at once.

The grains are small, so surface forces dominate over weight. They're irregular and sharp — never
rounded by wind or water — so they interlock mechanically with textured surfaces. And in a dry,
low-pressure environment they acquire and hold **electrostatic charge**, binding them electrically.

Low gravity makes all of this worse: the weight that would help a grain fall off is reduced, while
the adhesive forces are not.

Brushing and wiping — the obvious responses — are poor ones. They drive sharp grains into fabric and
seals, and abrade the very surfaces being cleaned.

The mechanism that actually suits the problem **exploits the same charge that causes it.**

An electrodynamic dust shield is a set of parallel electrodes embedded beneath a dielectric surface,
driven with multi-phase alternating voltage. That produces an electric field which *travels along the
surface as a wave*. The field charges the dust particles, and the travelling wave lifts and
transports them — marching them off the surface with no moving parts, no consumables, and no
contact. It was developed specifically for planetary dust mitigation and has been demonstrated on
relevant materials.

Mars hands you a second, independent handle: its dust is **strongly magnetic**, full of iron oxides,
so magnetic capture can collect what the electric sweep releases. Lunar dust is likewise magnetically
susceptible, through the same nanophase iron that makes it couple so well with microwaves.

Two different physical properties, both usable, is a better position than one.

## Why it has to be a stack

No single mechanism reaches zero, and designing as though one might is the characteristic failure of
dust control.

| Mechanism | Blind spot |
|---|---|
| Agitation / brushing | embeds fines; abrades |
| Gas jet | needs gas — none on the Moon |
| Electrodynamic sweep | complex shapes, deep fabric |
| Magnetic capture | everything non-magnetic |
| Directional airflow | carries, cannot clean |

Every mechanism has a *different* blind spot — which is exactly why they're worth stacking. The
residue of one is the target of the next.

Even stacked, the honest expectation is **asymptotic, not complete**. Some dust gets through every
cycle. The settlement's real defence is that the rate dust is *admitted* stays below the rate interior
systems *remove* it.

That reframes the design goal usefully: the gate isn't trying to achieve a clean transition. It's
trying to hold a budget.

## The best gate is the one nobody walks through

Which points at an architectural answer that outperforms every mechanism above.

A suit that **docks to the outside of the hull and is entered from within** — so its dusty exterior
never comes indoors at all — removes the largest single source of ingress by refusing to have the
problem. The seal gate stays necessary for cargo, rovers, and contingencies. But it shouldn't be the
primary line of defence if the architecture can avoid it.

The concept's trajectory is therefore unusual: **its long-term success is measured partly by how
seldom it gets used.**

## The honest difficulties

**The last fraction is the hard fraction.** Fine dust in fabric weave, seal grooves, threads, and
hinge gaps resists everything listed here. Ingress is continuous, and the settlement is always
removing dust rather than having excluded it.

**Cycle time bites soonest.** A decontamination sequence thorough enough to be useful takes time, and
a gate that adds a long delay to every egress quietly reduces how much work gets done outside — which
crews will route around if allowed to.

**Electrodynamic shields need power** and high-voltage drive electronics, and real-world performance
runs below laboratory figures because it depends on surface condition and particle properties.

**Seals are consumables** in a dusty environment, with a replacement schedule.

And on Mars the dust carries perchlorates — so the gate is handling a chemical hazard as well as an
abrasive one, which ties it straight to the scrubber concept.

None of this is a barrier of physics. The difficulty is that dust control is a permanent operating
burden, not a problem that gets solved once.

## Why it matters

Parts of this are ready now. The severity is documented from flight experience. Electrodynamic
shields have been built and tested for exactly this purpose. Martian dust's magnetism is established
from surface missions. Suitport architectures have been demonstrated in analogue testing.

The near-term step is the retrofit — electrodynamic panels and directional airflow added to an
existing airlock, with tracked-in dust actually *measured* before and after. Because the field's real
weakness isn't a shortage of mechanisms. It's a shortage of quantified operational data.

What this concept most needs isn't invention. It's measurement.
