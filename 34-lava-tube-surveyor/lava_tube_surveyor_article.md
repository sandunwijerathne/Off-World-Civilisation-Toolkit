# The Lava Tube Surveyor

### It would be a fine irony if the best shelter on the Moon turned out to be a hole that has been waiting there for three billion years.

Most of this catalogue is devoted to building shelter. Sintering domes out of dust.
Freezing shells of ice. Healing punctured hulls. All of it against the same enemies:
radiation, micrometeorites, and brutal temperature swings.

But the Moon and Mars may already possess shelters better than anything we could build.

Lava tubes form where a river of molten rock crusts over on top and the liquid beneath
drains away, leaving a hollow tunnel under a solid roof. They're common on Earth — and
there's now strong evidence for them elsewhere. Orbital imaging has found pits on the Moon
that open into subsurface voids, and gravity measurements from the GRAIL mission are
consistent with *large empty lava tubes* beneath the lunar maria. Because lunar gravity is
weak, these tubes could be far bigger than any on Earth — potentially kilometres across.
Similar features appear on Mars.

A tunnel beneath tens of metres of solid rock would be shielded from radiation and impacts
and would hold a nearly constant temperature. If these caves are as good as they look, the
best real estate off Earth may need no construction at all.

Only exploration.

## A small robot on a wire

The LT-1 "Ariadne" exists for one specific, difficult task: entering a lava tube through a
collapse pit — a *skylight* — and mapping what's inside.

It arrives at the rim aboard a rover or lander, anchors itself, and descends on a tether,
lowering past a vertical drop that may be tens of metres deep and past overhung walls no
wheeled vehicle could manage. The tether is its lifeline in every sense: it bears the drone's
weight, supplies its power, and carries its data back up — because radio will not propagate
out of a deep cave.

Once inside, it becomes a surveyor. It illuminates a place that has been in absolute darkness
since it formed, and scans its surroundings to build a three-dimensional map of the void: how
wide, how high, the shape of the floor, the direction it runs. It measures the radiation level
inside to confirm what the roof actually blocks. It records temperature to test whether the
expected stability is real. It samples the walls and examines the roof and rubble for signs of
weakness.

What it returns is the answer to the only question that matters: **is this cave a shelter we
could use, or a trap we should avoid?**

## Why the cave is worth the trouble

A lava tube offers for free most of what a built habitat struggles to provide.

Tens of metres of rock overhead is a formidable radiation shield against both cosmic rays and
solar particle events — as good as or better than the regolith and ice shielding the companion
concepts labour to assemble, and *already in place*. The same roof stops micrometeorites
outright. Underground, the punishing surface temperature swing gives way to something close to
constant, because rock conducts poorly and depth smooths the cycle. And the volume can be
immense — a large lunar tube could hold an entire settlement with room to grow.

The consequence is a different architecture entirely. Instead of building a pressure vessel and
then burying it under laboriously moved regolith, you could place a comparatively *light*
pressurised structure inside the tube and let the cave supply the shielding and the thermal
stability, while the structure supplies only the air.

The sintered dome, the ice shell, the self-healing skin — all become easier problems, or
unnecessary ones, when the hostile sky is replaced by a roof of solid basalt. That's why the
survey matters so much. The difference between a usable tube and an unusable one is the
difference between two very different plans for how to live on the Moon.

## The honest difficulty: nothing escapes a cave

Not light. Not radio. And not a machine that gets stuck.

Communication has to run down the tether or through relays, so a severed cable is
mission-ending, and the drone must act sensibly alone when it can't ask for help. The descent
is hazardous — rims may crumble, walls overhang, and the rubble of a collapsed skylight is
exactly the terrain most likely to snag a tether or trap a vehicle. Navigating in absolute
darkness means carrying your own light and your own frame of reference, with no satellite
positioning and no landmarks. Dust, cold, and the difficulty of recovery compound all of it.

There is a real possibility that a surveyor goes into a tube and simply never comes back.

None of this is a barrier of physics — the tubes appear to be there, and robotic cave
exploration is an active field on Earth. But this is genuinely dangerous robotics, and honesty
means accepting that some surveyors will be lost.

## Why it matters

The Lava Tube Surveyor is a small robot on a wire, lowered into a darkness that has never seen
light, carrying a lamp and a scanner and the question of whether people could live down there.

It may not come back. Some of the caves will prove useless. But the value of *knowing* is
enormous, because a settlement inside a lava tube would be shielded, steady, and spacious
without our building any of it.

Before we carry a civilisation to another world, it's worth sending one drone down a rope to see
whether the house is already standing.
