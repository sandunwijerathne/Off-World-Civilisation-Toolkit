# The Ballistic Cargo Capsule

### A catalogue full of elaborate machines for going up deserves one honest, stupid machine for coming down.

Every concept in this catalogue concerned with moving mass has been about going **up**. Mass
drivers, skyhooks, tugs, hoppers — all of them spending electricity or propellant to buy velocity,
because velocity is the expensive thing.

Coming down is a different problem with a completely different economy.

A vehicle arriving at a world carries an enormous amount of kinetic energy, and it has to get rid
of that energy before it can land. It can do that by running an engine — burning propellant hauled
all the way from somewhere else purely in order to slow down. Or, if the destination has an
atmosphere, it can do it by rubbing against air that was already there.

The asymmetry is huge and underappreciated. A propulsive landing on an airless body costs very
nearly as much velocity change as the launch did. An atmospheric entry costs almost none, because
the planet supplies the decelerating medium free of charge, in unlimited quantity, forever.

**The cheapest brake in the solar system is made of air.**

A logistics chain that understands this stops treating up and down as mirror images. Uphill you
build elaborate machines, because velocity is expensive. Downhill you build the simplest object
that can survive the trip, because the expensive part is being done for you.

## Deliberately unintelligent

The BC-1 "Trebuchet's Child" has no propulsion of its own.

Its forward face is a blunt heat shield sized to the entry conditions. Behind that, a simple cargo
shell. Behind that, parachutes — or a steerable parafoil where landing accuracy matters — and a
crushable base for touchdown. Its avionics amount to little more than sensing when to deploy what.

No engine. No wings. No landing legs. No ability to change its mind about where it's going once
released.

The name is deliberate. It's the *child* of the launcher: it doesn't get itself anywhere, and its
entire design assumes something else provided the velocity. A mass driver throws bulk cargo off a
low-gravity body; a tug moves it between orbits; and when that cargo needs to arrive somewhere with
air, this is what it rides down in.

Because it's cheap, dumb, and carries nothing it doesn't need, it can be made in quantity and —
depending on what its heat shield costs — either recovered and reflown or simply treated as
packaging. The economics are closer to a shipping container than to a spacecraft.

## Why blunt beats sharp

Here's the counterintuitive bit, and it's the heart of the whole concept.

Your instinct says a fast vehicle should be sharp and streamlined. For atmospheric entry that's
exactly backwards.

At hypersonic speed a vehicle forms a shock wave, and air crossing that shock is heated
enormously. A slender, pointed shape holds its shock tight against the body — which delivers that
heat straight into the vehicle's skin. A blunt shape does the opposite: it forces a strong,
*detached* bow shock to stand off in front of the vehicle, so most of the energy gets dumped into
the air instead of the structure, and only a fraction ever reaches the surface.

This is the blunt-body principle from Allen and Eggers' classic 1958 analysis, and it's why every
capsule that has ever come back from space looks like a badly-made bowl rather than a dart.

Two other quantities shape the trajectory. **Ballistic coefficient** — roughly mass divided by
drag — decides how deep you get before you slow down: a light, draggy capsule decelerates high up
where the air is thin and the heating is gentle, while a dense one drives deeper and suffers for
it. And **entry angle** defines a corridor with hard walls on both sides. Too steep, and heating
and deceleration exceed what the structure or the cargo can survive. Too shallow, and you skip
back out of the atmosphere like a stone off water.

With no engine, the capsule must thread that corridor by being *aimed correctly before release*.
It cannot correct afterwards.

## Where the trick works — and where it doesn't

**Earth:** the atmosphere does essentially the whole job. No propulsive braking at all — just
thermal protection and parachutes.

**Titan:** easier still. Dense atmosphere plus low gravity makes it, counterintuitively, the
gentlest place in the solar system to land.

**Moon and asteroids:** no atmosphere whatsoever. The capsule concept simply doesn't apply, and
every arrival gets paid for propulsively.

**Mars:** the awkward case — and awkward exactly where it matters most.

The Martian atmosphere is about one per cent of Earth's density. That's thick enough to generate
serious heating and demand a full heat shield, but far too thin to slow a heavy vehicle to a safe
landing speed on its own. A capsule that has done everything right is *still supersonic* when it's
uncomfortably close to the ground, leaving very little time and altitude for parachutes and final
descent.

This is the well-documented entry, descent and landing gap, and it gets **worse as vehicles get
heavier** — precisely the wrong direction for cargo. Mars gets a partial refund on braking, not a
free one, and any serious Martian cargo capsule has to pair aerodynamic entry with supersonic
retropropulsion or a large deployable decelerator.

## The honest difficulties

**The corridor is unforgiving and the capsule can't steer.** Everything depends on the release
conditions being right, which pushes the difficulty *upstream* onto whatever let it go — making
accurate aiming a requirement of the whole chain rather than of this vehicle.

**It lands in an ellipse, not at a point.** Shrinking that ellipse means adding a steerable
parafoil or guided entry, which is exactly the cost and complexity the concept exists to avoid.

**Thermal protection is the real cost.** An ablative shield is consumed and must be replaced; a
reusable one is heavier and more expensive to qualify. That choice decides whether the capsule is a
vehicle or packaging.

**The Mars gap is fundamental**, not incidental — and scales the wrong way.

And it's strictly one-directional: it solves arriving and contributes nothing to leaving.

None of this is a barrier of physics. Atmospheric entry is among the most thoroughly understood
problems in flight, settled in its essentials since the 1950s.

## Why it matters

This is the most flight-proven concept in the volume, which is both its strength and the reason it
needs no defending. Capsules have returned crews and cargo for decades; thermal protection is
mature; robotic landers have delivered to Mars repeatedly.

What doesn't exist yet is the capsule as a **commodity** — produced in quantity, released by shared
infrastructure, recovered as routinely as a shipping container, cheap enough that nobody thinks
about it. The near-term step is exactly that: standardise a cargo capsule to the same handling
interface the tug pool uses, so any mover can release it and any depot can stock it.

The engineering is done. The industrialisation is not.

Its shape is a lesson in itself — blunt, unlovely, deliberately draggy, because the fastest way to
shed energy is to refuse to be streamlined. Where there's atmosphere, arriving is nearly free, and
the right vehicle is the cheapest one that can take the heat. Where there isn't, no amount of
cleverness helps and the bill gets paid in propellant.

Knowing which world is which is the whole of logistics.
