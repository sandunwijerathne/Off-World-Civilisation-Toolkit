# The Vacuum Vapour Refinery

### The alembic is among the oldest instruments in human hands. What changes on the Moon is that the emptiness the alchemists could never achieve is simply there for the taking.

Most of this volume assumes a settlement can make things from local material: fibre from
basalt, structures from sintered regolith, oxygen from perchlorate. But nearly all of those
processes stop at ceramics and simple products.

A serious industry needs **metals** — iron, aluminium, titanium, magnesium — separated from
the rock they're locked into and purified enough to be useful. On Earth we do that with
chemistry: smelting with carbon, leaching with acids, electrolysis in molten salts, all
supported by enormous flows of water and reagents.

Off Earth, every one of those inputs is a problem. Carbon is scarce on the Moon. Acids and
solvents would have to be imported, contained, and recycled. Water is precious and needed for
life. A refinery that consumes imported chemistry is a refinery whose output is capped by
resupply — which is exactly the dependency local industry exists to escape.

What's needed is a separation process with **no consumables at all**: one whose only inputs
are ore and energy.

Distillation is such a process. And in vacuum, it becomes unusually practical.

## A still for rock

The VR-1 "Alembic" is a vacuum distillation plant for rock and metal.

Crushed feedstock — regolith, mineral concentrate, crude metal, or scrap — goes into a heated
crucible inside a chamber open to ambient vacuum. The charge is heated in stages. As the
temperature rises, each constituent in turn reaches the point where it vaporises, and the
vapour leaves the melt and travels to a train of collector surfaces held at controlled,
descending temperatures.

Each species condenses on the surface whose temperature suits it. So the vapours sort
themselves along the train: the least volatile condensing first and nearest, the most volatile
carrying furthest before settling.

What comes out is a set of separated deposits — the metals and compounds of the feedstock,
collected as condensed films or ingots, with the residue left behind in the crucible.

Nothing is consumed but power. No acid to neutralise. No flux to import. No water to recycle.
And no atmosphere to keep out, because the operating environment *is* the vacuum the process
requires.

## Why vacuum is the whole trick

A material boils when its vapour pressure equals the pressure around it — which is why water
boils cooler on a mountain than at sea level. In hard vacuum the surrounding pressure is
effectively zero, so materials vaporise at temperatures **far below** their familiar
atmospheric boiling points.

That's the entire basis of the concept. Vacuum lowers the thermal bar, so a process that would
demand extravagant temperatures at sea level becomes achievable with the heat a solar
concentrator or electric furnace can supply.

Separation follows because different species have different volatilities: at any given
temperature one constituent evaporates far faster than another, so raising the temperature in
steps releases the components roughly in order, and condensing them at different temperatures
sorts them again on arrival.

The physics is ordinary distillation, familiar from any chemistry laboratory. What's unusual is
the setting — which supplies for free the one condition that terrestrial vacuum metallurgy
spends heavily to create.

## Where it sits in the chain

Upstream of nearly everything.

The sintered structures, the basalt fibre, and the cast components of the companion concepts
use rock more or less as found. The vapour refinery is what turns rock into *materials* —
separated metals that can be alloyed, cast, formed, and machined into machinery.

It also does double duty as a recycler, because mixed scrap is an excellent distillation
feedstock: a jumble of alloys and broken components can be reduced to sorted metals by the same
process that refines ore. A settlement with a vapour refinery can make new metal from old, and
both from the ground.

## The honest difficulties

**It sorts only what will boil.** Species with very high boiling points — including some of the
most useful structural metals in their oxide forms — resist vaporisation even in vacuum unless
taken to extreme temperatures. So this is excellent at recovering the more volatile
constituents and poor at the most refractory ones. It complements other extraction routes
rather than replacing them.

**Energy bounds throughput.** Vaporising material in bulk demands enormous heat, so output is
capped by the power plant, and a settlement will refine slowly for a long time. Power, not
chemistry, paces the whole progression.

The hardware is genuinely difficult too: crucibles must survive molten contact at high
temperature, collectors must be held at controlled temperatures and then cleared of their
deposits, and vapour coats everything it touches — including the things you wish it wouldn't.
Separation is imperfect, so mixed deposits need further passes for purity.

None of this is a barrier of physics. Vacuum distillation is elementary, and the Moon supplies
the vacuum free. But the energy cost and the refractory limit are real.

## Why it matters

Heat a mixture, and its parts will leave in the order their natures dictate. That logic hasn't
changed since the first alembic.

What changes on the Moon is that the emptiness costs nothing — lowering every boiling point,
demanding no pump, no seal, no reagent to sustain it. The Vacuum Vapour Refinery takes that
free gift and turns crushed rock and broken scrap into sorted metal with nothing but heat.

It's slow, hungry for power, and it will always leave the most stubborn oxides behind. But it's
the machine that turns a place where people merely survive into a place where people can
*build* — and that's the difference between an outpost and an industry.
