# The Regolith Paved Airstrip

### A patch of fused regolith is a construction outcome. A facility is a promise.

The rail grader can fuse loose regolith into a hard crust, and one of its first products is a
landing pad.

This concept isn't about that machine. It's about the difference between *a patch of ground
somebody sintered* and *a facility a vehicle is cleared to land on* — which turns out to be a
difference of survey, tolerance, certification, and marking rather than of equipment.

So the RA-1 "Runway" is a specification, not a device.

## First, the name is wrong

The word in the title is borrowed, and taken literally it's nonsense.

A terrestrial runway is long because an aircraft has to accelerate along it until air moving over
its wings will carry its weight. On the Moon there's no air and therefore no lift, so nothing will
ever use a strip that way. On Mars the atmosphere is so thin that the speeds needed make winged
cargo operation impractical.

Every vehicle in this catalogue that arrives at a surface — the hopper freighter, a lander, a
returning payload — arrives **vertically, under thrust, on legs**.

It's worth being blunt about this, because the inherited word smuggles in the wrong mental model,
and the wrong mental model builds the wrong facility. What a surface installation actually has to
deliver has nothing to do with acceleration distance and everything to do with four measurable
things:

- a **bound area** wide enough that a descending plume doesn't excavate the ground
- a **crust** strong enough to carry a loaded footpad
- a **slope** shallow enough that a vehicle on legs won't tip
- a **geometry** somebody has surveyed and can certify

It's closer to a helipad and an apron than to an airport — and closer still to a standard than to
either.

## Why the bound area has to be big

The dominant hazard is plume-surface interaction, and its severity is a matter of *record*, not
estimate.

When a descending vehicle's exhaust hits unbound regolith, the gas accelerates surface particles
outward — and with no atmosphere to slow them, those grains travel on long, flat ballistic paths at
very high speed, scouring whatever's in the way.

The clearest demonstration is Apollo 12. It landed near Surveyor III, a spacecraft that had been
sitting quietly on the Moon since 1967. Parts of Surveyor were brought back to Earth, and they
showed clear erosion and pitting from the lunar module's plume, along with deposited material. A
landing sandblasts its neighbours at operationally significant distances.

Two consequences follow, and they set the facility's most important dimensions.

**The bound area must extend well beyond the vehicle's footprint**, because the hazard isn't where
the vehicle lands — it's where the ejecta *goes*. A small fused patch under the legs protects
nothing.

**The exclusion radius scales with thrust**, so it's sized to the largest vehicle expected, not the
typical one.

And the crust has to carry load. Sintered regolith is strong in compression, but it's a relatively
thin layer sitting on uncompacted soil, so the bearing rating depends on crust thickness and what's
underneath. A footpad that punches through a thin crust is arguably worse off than one that landed
on honest dust.

## What's actually in the specification

A table of properties with numbers attached — and every number traceable to the vehicle that has to
use the place:

| Specified property | Set by |
|---|---|
| Flatness tolerance | landing-gear stroke, tip-over margin |
| Slope limit | worst-case centre of mass |
| Bearing capacity | heaviest loaded footpad |
| Bound-area radius | largest plume |
| Exclusion radius | largest vehicle |
| Markings and reflectors | arriving sensor suite |
| Re-survey interval | degradation rate |

None of these can be chosen by the facility alone. Every one is a negotiation between the surface
and the fleet — which is exactly why the specification has to exist as a *document both sides agree
to*.

Then there's certification, which is the other half. A surveyed surface only means something
against a reference, and there's no satellite navigation off Earth to lean on. So the facility
carries its own **local geodetic network** — fixed monuments whose positions are known and
re-measured — against which flatness and slope get demonstrated, and re-demonstrated.

Thermal cycling, micrometeorite gardening, and repeated plume loading all degrade a surface. So
certification is recurring, not one-off.

The facility's real product isn't the pavement. It's the assurance, periodically renewed, that the
pavement is still within tolerance.

## The honest difficulties

**There's an awkward circularity.** The spec can't be written until the fleet exists; the fleet
can't be certified until the spec exists. That's ordinary in aviation but genuinely uncomfortable at
the start, and in practice it means the first facility gets built to one vehicle and generalised
afterwards, with all the retrofitting that implies.

**Surveying to tight tolerance without GPS is real work**, needing a monument network and instruments
that survive the environment.

**The crust is thin ceramic over loose soil**, so its bearing rating is harder to establish than a
terrestrial pavement's and varies across the site.

**Degradation is continuous**, making certification an operating cost rather than a capital one.

**Markings must work for machines, not eyes** — and survive vacuum, UV, and dust.

And the facility is expensive in the currency the grader is poorest in: *area*, and therefore energy.

None of this is a barrier of physics. The hazard is documented, the construction method
demonstrated. The difficulty is that this concept is mostly paperwork — and paperwork is the thing
engineering cultures are worst at treating as infrastructure.

## Why it matters

Unusually for this catalogue, the near-term step is a **document**, not a device.

The plume-erosion hazard is quantified from flight experience, including direct examination of
hardware sandblasted by a real lunar landing. Dust management has an established lessons-learned
literature. Regolith sintering is demonstrated.

What doesn't exist is an agreed specification stating what a lunar or Martian landing facility must
measurably provide, and how that gets verified. That could be drafted *now*, before any pad is
built — and doing it in that order is what would stop every early site from being bespoke. The
facility follows easily once the standard exists. The standard is much harder to write afterwards,
when incompatible pads are already in the ground.

The rail grader can deliver fused ground. Only a specification and a survey can deliver the promise
that the ground is this flat, will carry this much, was measured on this date, and won't throw its
own substance through your equipment when something lands on it.

The name "airstrip" will probably survive, because inherited words usually do. But nothing will ever
take off down it. What it provides instead is the least romantic thing in this volume and one of the
most necessary — a piece of ground somebody has measured, written down, and put their name to.
