# The Regolith Rail Grader

### Roads are the least celebrated and most consequential of technologies — the first thing a civilisation builds when it means to stay.

Every machine in this catalogue that moves across a planetary surface shares an adversary
that has nothing to do with vacuum, radiation, or cold.

It's dust.

Lunar regolith is made of grains shattered by billions of years of micrometeorite impact,
never rounded by wind or water, so they stay jagged and abrasive. They carry electrostatic
charge that makes them cling to everything. They're fine enough to work into any seal,
bearing, or joint. The Apollo crews found dust among the most serious practical problems
they faced — abrading suit fabric, jamming mechanisms, coating every surface, and resisting
every attempt to brush it off. And they were there for *days*. A settlement means to stay
for decades.

Here's the part that points to a solution: on a surface where nothing binds the ground, the
single biggest producer of disturbed dust is **traffic itself**. A rover crossing loose
regolith churns it, throws it, and — with no atmosphere to slow the ejecta — flings particles
on long ballistic arcs that coat equipment far away. Rocket landings are worse, blasting the
unbound surface into a sandblasting storm.

You can seal, filter, and clean. You should. But the more powerful answer is to stop making
the dust in the first place.

## A machine that cooks the ground into stone

The RG-1 "Appius" is a slow, heavy, self-propelled crawler that lays road by *transforming*
the ground beneath it rather than covering it.

It advances along the route in one continuous pass. At the front, a blade levels the surface
and clears rocks. Behind that, the prepared strip passes under a heating head that raises the
surface layer to the temperature where its grains soften and bond. Behind that, the fused
strip cools into a hard, coherent crust.

What comes out the back is a solid paved track where loose dust used to be — made entirely of
local ground, with no cement, no binder, and nothing imported at all.

The heat comes from one of two sources, and the choice shapes the machine. **Concentrated
sunlight**, gathered by mirrors, is essentially free energy on an airless world with no
weather. **Microwave heating** works regardless of illumination and couples remarkably well
with lunar soil, because its grains contain nanophase metallic iron that absorbs microwaves
strongly — so lunar regolith heats rapidly and *from within*, far more efficiently than
intuition suggests. A practical grader might carry both: solar for daytime economy, microwave
for the lunar night and for controlled depth.

## Sintered, not melted

The physics is sintering: bonding a powder into a solid by heating it *below* full melting.
Bring the grains to high temperature and material diffuses across the points where they touch,
welding them into a coherent load-bearing body while the bulk never becomes liquid. It's how
ceramics and many metal parts are made on Earth, and regolith — a powder of mineral grains and
glass — takes to it directly.

The result isn't concrete, because nothing is added. It's a fused ceramic crust: hard, strong
in compression, and above all *bound in place*, so it no longer produces dust.

## What the road is for

Three purposes, in roughly increasing urgency.

**Dust suppression** — a bound surface doesn't billow when driven on, so the traffic that was
the chief dust source becomes clean. **Trafficability** — a hard, level road lets vehicles go
faster, carry more, and use less energy than they can on loose sinking regolith, while sparing
wheels and drives from abrasion. And **landing pads** — a rocket landing on unbound regolith
excavates the surface and hurls debris outward at high speed, damaging everything nearby. A
fused pad largely eliminates that, and the hazard grows with vehicle size.

The same machine also makes **rail beds** — a sintered strip is a stable, load-bearing
foundation for rails between fixed points: mine to refinery, refinery to launcher. Including,
eventually, the long precisely-graded bed a mass driver needs.

## The honest difficulties

The governing constraint is **energy per unit area**. Sintering needs substantial heat and a
road is a very large area, so paving any real distance is slow and power-hungry. This is a
machine that creeps, and a settlement will have far less paved ground than it wants for a long
time.

The crust is ceramic — strong in compression, brittle in tension — so it may crack under
concentrated loads or thermal cycling, and it wants either a crack-tolerant design or
reinforcement of the kind the basalt fibre spinnery supplies. Achieving uniform depth and
quality over uneven, variable ground is hard. And the machine itself must survive doing hot,
dusty, abrasive work in vacuum for years — precisely the environment that destroys mechanisms.

None of this is a barrier of physics; regolith sintering is demonstrated and its microwave
coupling well characterised. These are the honest costs of paving a world with sunlight, one
slow strip at a time.

## Why it matters

On the Moon the case for roads is stronger than on Earth, because the loose ground isn't merely
inconvenient — it's actively hostile, an abrasive clinging dust that traffic lifts and scatters
over everything a settlement owns.

The Regolith Rail Grader answers it by cooking the ground into stone as it passes, converting
the enemy into the pavement, using nothing but sunlight or electricity and the dust already
underfoot. It's slow, it's hungry for power, and it will never pave as much as anyone wants.

But every strip it fuses is a strip that will never blow away again — and a settlement that has
bound the ground beneath its wheels has quietly solved the problem that would otherwise have
worn it down.
