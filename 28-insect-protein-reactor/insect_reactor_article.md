# The Insect Protein Reactor

### Of all the livestock we might carry to other worlds, the one that fits is the one we've spent history trying to keep out of the kitchen.

Plants can feed a crew calories and vitamins. What they struggle to supply is the
concentrated, high-quality animal protein and fat that has anchored human diets for all
of history.

On Earth we get that from livestock. And livestock are exactly what cannot come to space.

A cow is a tonne of animal that eats vast quantities of feed to make a little meat, drinks
enormous amounts of water, occupies great space, and grows over *years*. Every property
that makes cattle, pigs, and poultry viable on a broad-acred planet makes them impossible
in a habitat. You cannot bring a cow.

So the requirement is for an animal protein source that's small, fast, frugal with feed
and water, and — ideally — able to eat the waste a habitat already produces rather than
competing with the crew for food. There's such an animal. Actually, a whole class of them.

## The livestock that fits in a cupboard

The IP-1 "Gregor" is a compact, enclosed insect-farming system — a stack of trays in which
a colony of edible insects is reared on the habitat's organic waste.

Crop residues from the greenhouses — stems, leaves, trimmings, the roots the crew won't
eat — along with food scraps and other organic waste are prepared and fed to the insects.
The insects eat, grow, and multiply. At the right stage they're harvested and processed
into food — eaten whole, or milled into a protein-rich flour and blended into dishes. And
what they leave behind, their droppings (frass), is a nutrient-rich residue that fertilises
the crops. Another loop closed.

The species are ones already proven in terrestrial insect farming: black soldier fly larvae,
prized for devouring waste and converting it into fat and protein; crickets; mealworms. The
reactor just manages their environment — temperature, humidity, feeding, life stages — so the
colony sustains itself, continuously turning a stream of waste into a stream of protein.

## Why insects are the right animal

The case rests on efficiency, and it's striking.

Insects are cold-blooded, so they spend almost nothing keeping warm and instead convert a
large fraction of their feed into body mass. Many edible species yield far more edible
weight per unit of feed than cattle, with a fraction of the waste and greenhouse gas. They're
rich in protein, healthy fats, and micronutrients, comparing well nutritionally with
conventional meat. They breed prolifically and reach harvest in days or weeks. They need
little water and, stacked in trays, little space. And critically, many will thrive on organic
side-streams and waste — turning low-value material into high-value protein.

None of this is speculative. The UN's Food and Agriculture Organization has assessed edible
insects at length precisely as a sustainable, efficient source of protein for a crowded
world.

And every one of those advantages is *magnified* in a habitat. Feed efficiency matters most
where feed is scarcest. Frugality with water and space matters most where both are precious.
Fast growth suits a system that has to respond quickly. And the ability to eat waste is
transformative in a closed loop, because it turns the habitat's disposal problem into its
protein supply. The insect reactor doesn't just add food — it consumes waste and returns both
food and fertiliser, sitting right at the junction of the food and waste loops a settlement
must close.

## The honest catch: will they eat it?

The largest difficulty here isn't technical. It's human.

Many people are reluctant to eat insects, and a crew's willingness to accept insect-based
food — especially over *years* — is a genuine, non-trivial question. Processing the insects
into unrecognisable forms (flours and blended ingredients rather than visible bugs) helps a
lot, and cultural attitudes are shifting. But a food source the crew won't eat is no food
source at all, and this has to be taken seriously rather than waved away.

Beyond acceptance, the technical challenges are those of any closed animal system: keeping the
colony healthy without disease or collapse, preventing escape and contamination in a sealed
habitat, ensuring the insects and their feed carry no hazard, and maintaining the climate
control the colony needs. And how insects develop in microgravity isn't fully understood — an
active research question.

None of it is a barrier of biology; insect farming is established and endorsed on Earth. But
the acceptance question is real, and honesty means placing it first.

## Why it matters

The Insect Protein Reactor turns an old irony into an asset. It raises the small, fast, frugal
animals that eat our waste and give back protein — closing the food loop where a cow never
could, and disposing of organic waste in the same motion.

Whether a crew will embrace what it produces is the honest question at its heart, and the
answer will owe as much to cooking and culture as to engineering. But the biology is sound
and the efficiency is undeniable. A settlement that learns to eat as its circumstances demand
— lightly, frugally, without waste — will find that the humble insect was, all along, the most
spacefaring animal of them all.
