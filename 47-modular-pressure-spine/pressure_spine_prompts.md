# Modular Pressure Spine "Vertebra" — Image Generation Prompts

## Blueprint prompt

```
Technical engineering blueprint of the "MP-1 Vertebra," a modular pressure spine for a settlement,
drawn as a vintage cyanotype schematic: deep prussian-blue background, fine white and cyan
linework, faint drafting grid. Main view is a plan, captioned "modules never join each other — they
join the spine." A long horizontal corridor made of five identical rectangular segments runs across
the sheet, separated by bold vertical lines labeled "bulkhead," captioned "the spine — identical
segments; air · power · water · data in protected runs." Small circles along both long edges of the
corridor mark standard ports. Short connectors lead from these ports to rounded-rectangle modules
above and below: above, "lab," "quarters," "greenhouse," "galley," and one dashed outline labeled
"free port — plug in next"; below, "airlock," "medical," "stores," "workshop," "life support." A
dashed arrow extends from the corridor's end labeled "extend →." Beneath reads "one port geometry,
everywhere — the modules can be light, specialised, even experimental." Inset panel titled "why a
hole is 3× worse" showing a rectangular plate under tension from arrows at both ends, with a circular
hole in its centre and flow lines bending around it, two dots at the top and bottom of the hole
marked "σ ≈ 3× at the rim (Kirsch, 1898)"; below, a sharp-cornered opening labeled "sharp ✗" beside
a round-cornered one labeled "round ✓," captioned "tighter corner = higher peak stress, so round
every opening." Magnified detail circle showing a pressure-shell wall in section with a port opening,
thickened blocks at each side of the opening labeled "reinforced rim," a mating module flange below,
and small seal dots, captioned "one geometry · rounded · cycled to destruction once, in a tank,"
labeled "detail A — the one port." A bordered panel titled "the lesson written at the corners of
windows" recounting the 1954 de Havilland Comet failures, a fuselage cycled in a water tank until it
failed with fatigue cracks from the corners of openings, concluding "round every corner — break a
real one before trusting it." A second panel reads "! A STANDARD FREEZES A DECISION," and a small
comparison table contrasts bespoke joints with one spine. Numbered callout circles, a parts list,
and a title block marked "REV B." Monospaced drafting typography, flat orthographic projection, no
shading, no photorealism.
```

## Most-buildable real product prompt (Phase 1: full-scale spine segment cycled to destruction)

```
Photorealistic render of a structural qualification test inside a large aerospace test hall: a
full-scale pressurised habitat spine segment — a thick-walled aluminium cylinder several metres long
with two large circular side ports, each port ringed by a visibly thickened, machined reinforcing
collar with smoothly rounded edges — suspended horizontally inside an enormous open-topped steel
water tank. Dense bundles of strain-gauge wiring run from dozens of sensors bonded around the port
rims to a bank of data-acquisition racks on a gantry. Engineers in hard hats and hi-vis vests watch
screens showing stress plots and a cycle counter reading "CYCLE 48,117." Hydraulic pressurisation
lines and a large pressure controller connect to the segment's end bulkhead, stencilled "MP-1 QUAL
ARTICLE 01 — TEST TO FAILURE." Fine detail: rivet lines, surface-crack inspection markings drawn in
marker around one port corner, dye-penetrant stains, safety barriers, overhead crane, harsh
industrial lighting reflecting off the water surface. The mood is methodical and deliberate —
a structure being broken on purpose so it never breaks by surprise. Shot at a three-quarter angle
from the gantry, hyper-detailed, engineering documentary photography style, realistic and grounded.
```
