# The Regolith Sinter Dome

### The first true lunar building won't be delivered. It will be baked in place.

There's a number that governs everything about building on the Moon, and it's the
cost of a kilogram delivered to the lunar surface. Whatever that number is in any
given decade, it is always large enough that shipping construction material from
Earth is not a plan — it's a joke with a budget attached.

And a lunar base needs a *lot* of material. With no atmosphere and no magnetic
field, the surface takes the full weight of galactic cosmic rays, solar particle
storms, and a constant fine sleet of micrometeoroids. The only broadly effective
defence against all three is mass: metres of it, packed over everything you care
about. So the arithmetic is brutal and simple. You cannot bring the walls. You
have to make them there.

## The material is already everywhere

Fortunately, the Moon is covered in the stuff. Regolith — a deep blanket of
pulverised rock and glass, ground out of the crust by billions of years of
impacts — lies over the entire surface. It's abundant, it's free, and "mining" it
requires nothing more sophisticated than a scoop.

The problem has always been getting it to stick together. On Earth we bind
aggregate with cement, which needs water and chemistry that the Moon isn't
offering. So the Sinter Dome skips the binder entirely and fuses the regolith to
itself with heat — turning loose dust into solid ceramic where it lies, using
nothing but electricity and the ground underfoot.

The machine, the RS-1 "Hestia", is a crawler about the size of a small excavator
doing three things in a loop: scoop regolith, lay it as a shaped course along the
rising wall, and pass a microwave applicator slowly over it until the grains soften
and fuse. Then advance a few centimetres and do it again. Over weeks, a vault rises
out of the ground with no imported material, no formwork, and no human hands.

## A peculiarity of lunar dust

Here's the part that makes this genuinely promising rather than merely plausible.

Sintering — heating a powder until its particles bond without fully melting — is
ancient technology; it's how ceramics work. Any silicate dust can be sintered in
principle. But lunar regolith turns out to be *unusually* good at it, for a reason
specific to the Moon.

All those micrometeorite impacts have vapour-deposited a coating of nanophase
metallic iron onto the surfaces of the soil grains. And that iron couples strongly
with microwaves. Taylor and Meek's experimental work found that real lunar soil
heats to melting in a modest microwave field with startlingly little power —
dramatically more readily than terrestrial rock or the simulants we test with on
Earth.

The upshot: the energy budget for fusing regolith on the Moon is far more forgiving
than Earth-based intuition suggests. The Moon spent four billion years, entirely by
accident, pre-treating its dust to be easy to microwave.

## The part everyone gets wrong

Now the honest bit, because descriptions of sintered lunar domes usually skip it
and it matters enormously.

A masonry dome works because gravity presses its material together. The shell sits
in compression, and fused regolith — like stone, brick, and concrete — is strong in
compression. Beautiful. Ancient. Reliable.

But a *pressurised habitat* is the exact opposite problem. Internal air pressure
pushes outward, which puts the shell into **tension** — and ceramics are weak in
tension. Worse, lunar gravity is a sixth of Earth's, so the shell's own weight, the
very thing that would normally hold it in compression, is feeble.

A bare sintered dome, pressurised to anything breathable, would not hold. It would
try to lift off its own foundation and split.

There are two honest fixes, and a serious design uses both. Keep the pressure load
off the ceramic entirely — the dome is shielding and structure, while a separate
flexible or metallic liner inside it, anchored independently, actually holds the
air. And bank loose regolith over the finished dome as ballast, so the weight of
the overburden keeps the shell in net compression despite the pressure inside,
which has the elegant side effect of adding exactly the radiation shielding you
needed anyway.

The dome is a shield and a shell. It is not, by itself, a pressure vessel. Claiming
otherwise is where these concepts usually go wrong.

## Start with the parking lot

The staging is deliberately unglamorous, and that's the strength of it.

Phase one isn't a dome at all — it's landing pads and roads. Rocket exhaust hitting
loose lunar dust throws it outward at enormous speed, sandblasting everything
nearby. It's a serious, well-documented hazard, and a fused hardened pad is among
the first pieces of infrastructure any lunar base genuinely needs. It also requires
zero structural sophistication. You're just melting the ground flat.

Phase two adds berms, retaining walls, and shielding banked around existing
modules — still pure compression, still structurally simple. Phase three raises the
actual vaults, fits the pressure liner, and buries the result.

Each phase delivers something useful on its own. Each earns the next.

## What it means

Every civilisation before ours built its first shelters out of whatever the ground
gave it — mud, stone, ice, turf. There's no reason the next one should be
different.

The Regolith Sinter Dome takes the cheapest material in the solar system, the dust
we'd otherwise merely curse for wrecking our machines, and turns it into pavement
and walls and vaults with nothing but electricity and a four-billion-year
accident of lunar chemistry.

It won't hold air by itself. That limitation shapes the entire design, and pretending
otherwise helps nobody. But it will hold back the radiation, the micrometeoroids,
and the blasted dust of every landing — without a single kilogram lifted from Earth.
