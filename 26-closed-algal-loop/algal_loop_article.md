# The Closed Algal Loop

### The machines that let us live among the stars may be less like engines than like gardens. The first one is a tank of algae.

A crew far from Earth cannot keep buying its air.

Every breath consumes oxygen and produces carbon dioxide. A mission that resupplies
those from home is tethered to home. A mission that scrubs the carbon dioxide out and
throws it away wastes both the carbon *and* the effort. Neither works for the journeys
and settlements worth caring about — a crew a year from home, or a base meant to last
decades, cannot depend on a supply line for the very air it breathes.

The living world solved this problem billions of years ago, and it solved it with
photosynthesis. In the great planetary loop, animals breathe in oxygen and breathe out
carbon dioxide; photosynthetic organisms breathe in that carbon dioxide and breathe out
oxygen, powered by light. The two halves balance, and the air stays alive.

A spacecraft is an incomplete biosphere. It has the animal half — the crew — but not the
photosynthetic half. This concept supplies the missing half in its most compact form: a
tank of microalgae.

## A forest in a tank

The AL-1 "Chlorella" is a photobioreactor — an illuminated vessel holding a dense green
suspension of microalgae in water, through which the cabin air is circulated. Bathed in
light, the algae take up the carbon dioxide the crew exhales and, by photosynthesis,
release oxygen back into the air stream. It's the same gas exchange a forest performs,
concentrated into a tank.

As they work, the algae grow and multiply, so a portion of the culture is continuously
harvested to keep the population steady — and that harvest is itself a resource, because
many microalgae are edible and nutritious. The machine that renews the air also yields
food.

Why algae rather than crops? Because for the specific job of closing the air loop in the
least volume, they win on every axis: they grow far faster than plants, exchange gas more
efficiently per unit volume, live in a compact controllable tank instead of beds of soil,
and can be pumped, dosed, and harvested like a fluid rather than tended like a garden.
Higher plants — with their food and their psychological comfort — come later and
alongside. The algal loop is the compact core that keeps the air alive.

## This is old science

The best thing about this concept is that almost none of it is new.

Photosynthesis needs no defending — it's the operating principle of every ecosystem on
Earth. And the idea of harnessing it for life support has a long, serious pedigree. The
Soviet **BIOS-3** facility in the 1970s and 80s sealed human crews inside a closed
environment where algae and plants regenerated air and water and produced part of the
food, keeping people breathing for *months*. The European Space Agency's **MELiSSA**
project has pursued the same goal for decades, building an engineered, multi-compartment
micro-ecosystem with photosynthetic organisms at its heart.

The Closed Algal Loop isn't a departure from that lineage — it's a distillation of it:
the photosynthetic compartment, built as a stand-alone air-renewing machine.

## The honest part: living things can die

A living life-support system trades the predictability of chemistry for the resilience —
and the fragility — of biology.

A culture can crash. Infection, a toxin, a control failure, a swing in temperature or
light can sicken or kill the algae, and a life-support system that can *die* is one that
has to be buffered by reserves and, for a long time, backed up by a conventional system.
You do not remove the chemical scrubbers until the living replacement has thoroughly
earned your trust.

And closing the loop *fully* is genuinely hard. BIOS-3 and MELiSSA represent decades of
effort between them precisely because balancing every input and output of a self-contained
ecosystem — trace nutrients, waste products, the exact match of gas produced to gas
consumed — is far more intricate than closing the oxygen-carbon exchange alone. There's
also a specific space problem: in microgravity, gas and liquid don't separate the way they
do under gravity — bubbles don't rise, cultures don't settle — so the reactor has to manage
its mixing and gas exchange actively rather than relying on buoyancy.

None of this is a barrier of biology or physics — the loop runs on Earth every day. But a
fully closed, reliable, space-rated version is a decades-long engineering programme, not a
single gadget, and it's only honest to say so.

## Why it matters

Every breath a crew takes is a small withdrawal from a finite account, and you cannot live
off-world by carrying the whole account with you.

The Closed Algal Loop repays the account continuously — handing the crew's exhaled carbon
to a tank of living green cells and taking back the oxygen those cells release, exactly as
the living world has done since before there were animals to breathe. It won't close
perfectly at first, and the honest path keeps a conventional system alongside it for a long
while. But every fraction of the air it renews is a fraction the settlement no longer has
to buy.

In the end, the first of humanity's off-world gardens will be a tank of algae — quietly
turning yesterday's breath back into tomorrow's.
