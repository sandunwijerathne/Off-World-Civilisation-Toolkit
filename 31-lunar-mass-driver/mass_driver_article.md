# The Lunar Mass Driver

### The moment the Moon stops being only a destination and becomes a source — flinging its wealth outward on a beam of electric force.

Every rocket that has ever flown has been shackled by the same arithmetic. To gain speed it
throws propellant backward — and to carry that propellant it needs *more* propellant, so the
fuel required grows steeply with the speed you want. The result is that a rocket leaving Earth
is almost entirely fuel, with only a sliver of payload perched atop a towering tank.

That's the tyranny of the rocket equation, and it's why launching from Earth is so expensive.
On Earth we have little choice — deep gravity and thick atmosphere leave few alternatives.

The Moon is different in exactly the ways that matter. Its gravity is about a *sixth* of
Earth's, so the speed needed to leave its surface is far lower. And it has no atmosphere, so
there's no air resistance, no need for streamlining, no thickest-air to climb above.

On a world like that, a completely different launch method becomes practical: instead of
carrying energy as fuel, leave the energy on the ground as *electricity*, and use it to
accelerate the cargo along a fixed track by magnetic force — releasing it at the modest speed
the Moon's weak gravity requires. You break the rocket's tyranny not with a better rocket, but
by dispensing with the rocket altogether.

The machine that does this is the mass driver.

## A catapult made of magnets

The Lunar Mass Driver is a long, straight electromagnetic accelerator — a catapult that uses
magnetism instead of tension.

A payload sits in a carrier, or *bucket*, that runs along a track lined with electromagnets. As
the bucket passes, the magnets switch on in sequence to pull it ever faster, building speed over
the length of the track until the payload is moving fast enough to coast away from the Moon. At
the end of the track the payload releases and flies free, while the bucket is braked and returned
to the start to be loaded again. No part of the launch energy leaves with the cargo — it's all
delivered electrically, from the track, during the run.

The physics is entirely conventional: it's the same electromagnetic acceleration that drives
maglev trains and the electromagnetic catapults now launching aircraft, scaled up to launch
speed. And because speed accumulates with distance, a longer track means a *gentler*
acceleration reaches the same launch speed — the trade between track length and acceleration is
the central design choice.

On the Moon the numbers are forgiving: the speed to reach lunar orbit is a small fraction of
what it takes to leave Earth, and with no atmosphere the payload loses nothing to drag and needs
no heat shielding. The launch speed at the end of the track is very nearly the speed the payload
keeps. There are no unknowns here — only engineering at scale.

## What it's for (and what it isn't)

The limits define the tool, and they're worth stating plainly.

The accelerations needed to launch from a track of practical length are far too high for people
or fragile equipment. So the mass driver is strictly a **freight launcher for durable cargo** —
raw regolith, processed metals, blocks of material, propellant, anything rugged that doesn't
mind being flung. Its output is a stream of ballistic packages that have to be *caught* at the
far end, which means a receiving system in orbit. And it launches only *outward*; it does
nothing for landing, which needs other means.

Within those limits it's extraordinarily powerful. Once built and powered, it throws cargo off
the Moon again and again at the cost only of electricity and the wear of the machine, with no
propellant consumed — a launch economy no rocket can approach.

## The honest difficulties

The challenges are engineering and scale, not physics.

Delivering the large, precisely-timed pulses of power each launch demands is hard, as is
supplying the average power for a high launch rate. The release must be clean and the aim exact,
because a payload coasting ballistically can't correct itself — small errors at release become
large errors downrange, so the launcher must be finely controlled and paired with a catcher in
orbit. The accelerations rule out people and fragile goods entirely. Building and aligning a
long, precise track on the lunar surface, and maintaining it against dust and thermal extremes,
is a serious construction task.

And the whole scheme is useful only where there's something worth launching in quantity, and
something to catch it. It's infrastructure for an *established* lunar industry, not a first step.

None of this is a barrier of physics — the launch principle is the same one maturing in
terrestrial electromagnetic launchers, and the lunar application was worked out in quantitative
detail back in the 1970s as the linchpin of using the Moon's resources.

## Why it matters

The rocket's great weakness is that it must lift the means of its own ascent — and on a small,
airless world, that weakness can simply be set aside.

The Lunar Mass Driver leaves the energy on the ground, dispenses with propellant altogether, and
throws cargo off the Moon with nothing but electricity and magnetism, again and again, for the
price of the power it draws. It can't launch people and it can't land anything, and it's only
worth building once the Moon has goods to ship. But for the steady export of rugged lunar
material — the metal, the regolith, the propellant a spacefaring economy will want in quantity —
it offers a launch cost no rocket can touch.

It's the Moon's loading dock: the moment it stops being only a place we go, and becomes a place
that sends things out.
