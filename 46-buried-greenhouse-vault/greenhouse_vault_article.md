# The Buried Greenhouse Vault

### The picture everyone carries of farming on another world is the one arrangement a radiation environment forbids.

A greenhouse and a shelter want opposite things.

A shelter wants mass overhead — regolith, ice, rock — to stop radiation and micrometeorites. A
greenhouse wants light, which means transparency, which means a large area of the structure is
deliberately *not* shielded.

So the popular image of an off-world farm — a glazed dome full of green under an alien sky — is a
picture of a radiation hole with crops in it. And the crew tending it is standing inside the one
part of the settlement that was left open on purpose, which is also the part people would most want
to linger in.

Every other habitat concept in this catalogue works in one direction: get mass between the crew and
the sky. Then agriculture shows up and asks for the opposite.

## Take the window apart

The contradiction dissolves once you notice that a window does **two separate jobs**.

It *gathers* light from outside. And it *delivers* that light to the inside. It does both at the
same place — which is the only reason it has to be a hole.

There's no physical requirement that those jobs happen in the same place.

Collect the light on the surface, using a mirror or a panel that nobody needs to stand near. Carry
it downward through a penetration far smaller than a window. Release it inside a fully buried
volume. The shielding stays intact except for a modest duct, the crops get their photons, and the
crew works underground.

That's the GV-1 "Demeter": a pressurised growing volume under roughly two metres of regolith, with
no transparent roof and no view of the sky. Above it sits the collection plant — a tracking
concentrator feeding optical fibres, or a PV array feeding lamps below, and realistically both.

## The overburden pays three times

This is what makes burial *attractive* rather than merely tolerable.

**It shields** — protecting both the crew working inside and the genetic continuity of seed stock
across many generations.

**It's thermal mass** — holding the vault steady while the surface above swings through its
enormous day-night range. That matters enormously to growing plants and to the machinery serving
them.

**It's structural ballast** — a pressurised chamber wants to burst outward, and two metres of
regolith pressing down is a substantial part of the load path holding the roof in place. Same logic
that makes the sintered dome work in compression rather than tension.

## Plants don't want all the light you'd give them

Sunlight is broadband. Photosynthesis is not.

Plants absorb strongly in the blue and red and reflect a large part of the green — which is
precisely why they look green to us. A substantial fraction of the energy in raw sunlight does no
photosynthetic work at all. What a crop needs is enough photon flux *in the useful band* over
enough hours a day. Everything outside it is heat to be removed rather than growth to be gained.

This flips the apparent economics of lighting.

Piping sunlight down a fibre keeps light as light and avoids conversion losses, which sounds
decisively efficient — but it delivers the *whole* spectrum, including the part the crop won't use
and the infrared the vault then has to reject.

Converting sunlight to electricity and back to photons pays a conversion penalty — but lets you
confine the emitted light to the wavelengths plants actually absorb. And LEDs are efficient,
long-lived, controllable, and cool enough to sit close to foliage, which is why they've become the
standard tool in controlled-environment plant research.

| | Piped sunlight (fibre) | LEDs from surface power |
|---|---|---|
| Conversion losses | low — light stays light | PV → electric → photon |
| Spectrum | everything, useful or not | tuned to what plants absorb |
| Reach | limited by run and bends | anywhere a cable goes |
| Through the night | nothing to deliver | works, if power is stored |
| Failure mode | passive, robust | electronics in a warm, wet place |

The comparison is genuinely close and depends on crop, distance, and whether you must grow through
a long night. A serious vault will use both: piped daylight while the sun is up, lamps to extend
the photoperiod and carry the crop through darkness.

## The light budget is the whole problem

Once the shielding contradiction is resolved, what's left is arithmetic — and it's unforgiving.

A crop needs a certain photon flux, over a certain area, for a certain number of hours. Multiply
those and you get an energy demand that scales directly with how much food you want. Controlled-
environment agriculture has been studied for decades precisely because *this budget*, not the
biology, is the binding constraint. Plants grow perfectly well in engineered environments. The
question has always been what it costs to light and service them.

Burial makes that budget harder in one way and easier in another. Harder, because every photon must
now be collected, transported, and delivered, and each step loses some. Easier, because underground
thermal stability removes most of the heating and cooling load a surface greenhouse would fight
continuously, and the vault never has to survive the thermal and micrometeorite environment that
would dominate the design of any large glazed structure.

It trades an optical cost for a structural and thermal saving. Whether that's favourable depends on
the site — which is an honest way of saying it has to be computed, not assumed.

## The honest difficulties

**Energy binds everything else.** A farm large enough to feed people is a major power consumer, and
on the Moon it has to be fed through a fourteen-day night — meaning either enormous storage or
accepting that the crop cycle pauses. Plants don't pause gracefully.

**Buried volume is expensive per cubic metre**, pushing the design toward vertical stacking and
high-yield crops, and ruling out the spacious, pleasant greenhouse of the popular image.

**Optical transport is lossy** over distance and intolerant of tight bends, constraining the
geometry of collector and vault.

**A warm, humid, pressurised chamber** is unkind to electronics and friendly to moulds and
pathogens, which in a closed system have nowhere else to go.

And **burial removes the view** — the psychological benefit that growing things under an open sky
was supposed to provide. That's a real loss, even if it isn't an engineering one.

None of this is a barrier of biology. The difficulty is paying for the photons.

## Why it matters

The components are all in hand and several are commercial. Controlled-environment agriculture under
artificial light is an established industry. LEDs tuned to plant absorption bands are the standard
tool. Fibre-optic daylighting exists in terrestrial buildings. And burial is the simplest
construction in the whole catalogue.

The near-term step is the phase-one lamp-lit salad vault — no optical infrastructure needed, just a
buried pressurised volume and spare power.

What should be resisted is the glazed surface dome. It photographs beautifully and shields nothing.

The vault is darker and less romantic than the dome. It's also the version that lets people stand
among the crops for years rather than hours.
