# The Thermal Mass Battery Wall

### A settlement's first need through a fourteen-day night isn't power. It's not freezing.

The Moon's day and night each last about fourteen Earth days.

For two weeks the sun delivers energy in abundance. For the next two it delivers nothing at all,
and with no atmosphere to hold warmth, the surface radiates its heat straight to space. At the lunar
equator, surface temperatures swing from well above water's boiling point at noon to far below
freezing through the night — one of the largest thermal cycles on any body we could plausibly
settle.

Almost every concept in this catalogue has run into that night. The kiln stops when the sun does.
The buried greenhouse has to light its crops through the dark or let them stop. Solar power
vanishes at sunset.

The obvious answer is chemical batteries — but a battery bank big enough to carry a whole
settlement through two weeks is vast, heavy to import, and, inconveniently, degraded by exactly the
cold it exists to defeat.

What's needed is a store that's cheap in mass, made locally, and indifferent to temperature. Heat
stored in rock is all three. The Romans heated their bathhouse floors this way, passing hot air
under stone. The name comes from them.

## Bank the afternoon in stone

The TB-1 "Hypocaust" is a thermal store built into a settlement's structure.

Its **core** is a large mass of dense, processed regolith — sintered blocks or cast basalt from the
machines elsewhere in this catalogue — laid as a wall, a floor, or a buried bed beside the habitat.

Through the lunar **day**, the core gets charged: heat from a concentrator, waste heat from the kiln
and refinery, or surplus electricity flows in through embedded heat pipes and raises its
temperature.

Through the **night**, the flow reverses. Heat drawn from the core keeps the habitat, its water, its
machinery, and its crops above the temperatures where they fail.

Around the core sits an insulating **jacket** of loose, unprocessed regolith — heaped thickly and
left alone. And this isn't a compromise. It's the other half of the design, and it costs almost
nothing.

## One material, two states

Here's the bit of physics that makes the whole thing work — and at first glance it looks like a
paradox.

The energy a body stores as heat is mass × specific heat × temperature change. Regolith's specific
heat is modest, but its mass is free, so you buy capacity with tonnes instead of chemistry. Storing
heat isn't the hard part. **Moving** it is.

And here's the twist: **loose regolith in vacuum is an extraordinary insulator.**

On Earth, heat crosses a granular material mostly through the air between grains. In vacuum there's
no air — so heat can only cross through the tiny contact points between grains and by radiation
across the gaps. Very little gets through.

The Apollo heat flow experiments measured the consequence directly: the enormous temperature swing
at the lunar surface is damped out within roughly the first metre of depth. Below that, the
temperature barely notices whether it's noon or midnight.

That's wonderful for *keeping* heat in. It's useless for getting heat *into or out of* a store
quickly.

Now sinter or melt that same regolith. Fusing it closes the gaps between grains and turns it into a
continuous solid, raising its conductivity enormously. Heat can move through it.

So the design uses the material twice:

| | Dense core | Loose jacket |
|---|---|---|
| Made from | sintered / cast regolith | regolith as found |
| Conductivity | high — heat moves | tiny in vacuum — heat stays |
| Job | store, charge, discharge | keep the heat in |
| Cost | processing energy | almost nothing |
| Made by | grader, kiln, spinnery | a shovel |

**Dense where heat has to move. Loose where heat has to stay.** One substance, two states — and the
performance depends on getting the boundary between them right.

## The honest limit: heat wants to stay heat

A thermal store is superb at supplying heat and mediocre at supplying electricity, and that
distinction decides where it's worth using.

Keeping a habitat warm, keeping water liquid, keeping bearings and electronics above their minimum
operating temperature — those are *heat* demands, and a thermal battery meets them directly, with
almost no loss beyond what leaks through the jacket.

Turning stored heat back into electricity is a different story. Any heat engine is limited by the
temperature difference it works across. A store charged to a modest temperature, discharging
against a cold environment, can convert only a fraction of its energy into work — the rest has to be
rejected. Charge it hotter and that fraction improves, but so do the demands on the core, the pipes,
and the insulation.

So match the store to the demand. A settlement needs a *great deal* of heat through the lunar night
— arguably more than it needs electricity — and every joule of that supplied straight from the store
is a joule not pulled from a chemical battery or a reactor.

The thermal battery doesn't replace electrical storage. **It removes the largest single load from
it.**

## The honest difficulties

**The store has to be big.** Rock isn't a dense energy store by chemistry's standards, so carrying a
whole settlement through two weeks means processing and placing a lot of material. And the energy
spent sintering the core is a real cost that has to be recovered over many cycles before the store
pays for itself.

**Heat transfer is the engineering crux.** Heat pipes and fluid loops embedded in rock have to
survive repeated thermal cycling, mustn't freeze or leak, and must move heat fast enough through a
material whose conductivity — far better than loose regolith's — is still only modest.

**The jacket only works undisturbed.** Anything that compacts or churns it degrades the insulation.

**Thermal cycling stresses the core itself**, which is a ceramic and prone to cracking under repeated
expansion and contraction.

**The conversion limit is fundamental.** Use it to generate electricity and it'll disappoint anyone
expecting it to behave like a chemical battery.

None of this is a barrier of physics — regolith's thermal behaviour is measured, not modelled. The
difficulties are scale, heat transfer, and asking the store only for what it's good at.

## Why it matters

Thermal storage in rock and masonry is one of the oldest energy technologies there is — from the
Roman hypocaust to modern solar-thermal plants storing heat in molten salt or packed beds of stone.

The near-term step is a small **survival store**: a modest block of sintered regolith beside a
habitat, sized only to keep critical systems alive through one lunar night — and above all,
*instrumented*. Because the numbers that actually matter are the real charge rate, the real
discharge rate, and the real leakage through a real jacket over a real lunar night. Those three
numbers decide how big every later store has to be, and they can only be learned on the Moon.

The lunar night is the single most repeated problem in this catalogue. The Thermal Mass Battery Wall
answers with the oldest trick in heating: bank the afternoon's warmth in stone and spend it after
dark. What makes it work on the Moon is a lucky piece of physics — the ground is a poor conductor
when loose and a good one when fused, so a settlement can build both the store *and* its insulation
from the dirt it's standing on.

For not freezing through a fourteen-day night, there's no cheaper answer than a hot rock in a heap
of cold dust.
