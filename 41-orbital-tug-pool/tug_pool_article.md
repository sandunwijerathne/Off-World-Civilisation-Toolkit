# The Orbital Tug Pool

### Cities didn't solve urban transport by giving everyone a better car.

Consider how strange spaceflight logistics would look applied to any other transport domain.

Every shipper builds its own ship. Each one is sized for that shipper's largest conceivable cargo,
sails once, and is then scuttled or parked. No shipper may borrow another's vessel, because no two
vessels share a cargo fitting. The capital cost of the whole industry gets paid repeatedly, by
everyone, for capacity that sits idle between voyages.

That is very nearly the present arrangement above the atmosphere — and it's a reasonable one only
while flights are rare.

The moment traffic becomes routine, it collapses under its own waste. Once mass drivers are
throwing cargo off the Moon, depots are storing propellant, and foundries in orbit are waiting on
feedstock, what the economy needs isn't better rockets. It needs a different *ownership model*: a
modest number of reusable movers, held in common, kept busy.

Taxis, not private cars.

## A deliberately boring machine

The OT-1 "Mule" is a reusable orbital transfer vehicle built to spend its entire life in vacuum.

No wings. No heat shield. No landing legs. No aerodynamic shaping — because it never enters an
atmosphere or touches a surface. What it has is propulsion, tankage, a standardised docking and
grapple interface at each end, power, thermal control, and enough autonomy to rendezvous without
someone watching it the whole time. It collects a payload at one orbit, carries it to another,
releases it, and goes to its next job or to a depot to refuel.

The tug itself is intentionally dull. **The pool is the concept.**

Instead of every operator owning a transfer stage, a shared fleet is dispatched against a queue of
transfer requests, the way a haulage company dispatches lorries. A tug that has just delivered to a
high orbit doesn't come back empty if something needs bringing down. A tug low on propellant gets
routed via a depot instead of being thrown away. The fleet gets sized to the *aggregate* demand of
the whole economy rather than to everyone's individual peak.

## Delta-v is the currency

Moving between orbits isn't about distance. It's about energy — and the price is paid in velocity
change.

Two orbits around the same body differ in energy, and you move between them by changing speed at
the right moments: one burn to raise the far side of your orbit, a second to circularise once you
arrive. That's the transfer worked out in Hohmann's foundational 1925 analysis, and the total
velocity change required is the currency in which every orbital task is priced. A tug's capability
is simply how much of it the vehicle can deliver, to how much payload.

*How* you produce that velocity change is the central trade, and it splits the fleet in two.

**Chemical** propulsion gives large thrust for a short time. Transfers take hours or days — good
for urgent or crewed payloads — but it burns a lot of propellant.

**Electric** propulsion gives tiny thrust continuously and throws its propellant out far faster, so
it needs dramatically less of it for the same velocity change. The cost is transfers measured in
weeks or months, plus a large power supply to run the thrusters.

Neither wins outright. A realistic pool carries both: fast chemical tugs for the urgent and the
crewed, slow electric tugs for bulk cargo that doesn't care when it arrives — which, in an
industrial economy, is most of it.

## Why pooling is the whole point

Reusability only pays if the reusable thing is actually *used*.

A vehicle flying twice a year has to recover its cost over two flights. The same vehicle flying
thirty times recovers it over thirty. Since the fixed cost of designing, qualifying, and building a
tug dwarfs the marginal cost of one transfer, utilisation is the variable that decides whether
orbital transfer is expensive or cheap. And no single operator generates enough traffic to keep a
dedicated tug busy.

Pooling fixes that by aggregating demand — and it throws off second-order benefits that matter more
than they first look:

- A shared fleet justifies **spares and redundancy** no individual operator could afford.
- It creates a standing reason for a **common docking interface**, which makes every payload in the
  economy compatible with every mover. That's the moment a standards bureau stops being bureaucracy
  and becomes infrastructure.
- It turns transfer capacity into a **market** — something priced, scheduled, and traded, rather
  than rationed by whoever happens to own a stage.

The tug pool is less a machine than an institution with engines attached.

## The honest difficulties

**Refuelling is the pivot, and it isn't routine yet.** Transferring propellant between vehicles in
free fall — especially cryogenic propellant, which boils away and won't settle in a tank without
gravity — has been studied far more than it's been practised. A tug that can't be refuelled is just
an expensive single-use stage with extra steps.

**Standardisation is institutional, not technical.** A shared fleet needs a common docking and
grapple interface that everyone adopts, which means agreement before hardware — and agreement is
historically slower than engineering.

**Electric tugs carry a specific penalty.** Weeks spent spiralling through radiation belts is
damaging to sensitive payloads, ruling them out for some cargo entirely, quite apart from the
schedule.

**Shared ownership raises real questions** of liability, priority, and who answers when a tug fails
with someone else's payload aboard.

And **timing matters**: the pool is only worth building where traffic already exists. Deployed too
early it's a fleet of idle vehicles — precisely the waste it was meant to cure.

None of this is a barrier of physics. Orbital transfer is the best-understood manoeuvre in
spaceflight, and both propulsion families fly today. The difficulties are propellant handling,
agreement, and timing.

## Why it matters

The rocket equation has taught spaceflight to think about *vehicles*, when the problem in a working
economy is *traffic*.

A tug is an unremarkable machine — tanks, thrusters, a grapple, and enough sense to find its own way
to a rendezvous. Deliberately so, because the innovation isn't the hardware. It's refusing to let
the hardware sit idle.

Held in common, kept busy, refuelled rather than discarded, a modest fleet of dull vehicles can move
more mass more cheaply than a sky full of privately-owned stages each waiting on its owner's next
launch.

It's the least glamorous concept in this volume and possibly the most consequential. The moment
orbital transfer becomes something you *buy* rather than something you *build*, everything else in
the catalogue gets cheaper.
