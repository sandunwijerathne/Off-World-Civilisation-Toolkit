# The Mycelial Digestion Vat

*Category: Life Support & Agriculture · Off-World Civilisation Toolkit · Concept 50*

The water plant takes the liquid. This one takes what's left.

Every machine in this volume that grows something also makes something nobody can eat. Stems, roots, leaves, husks — the harvest index, the ratio of edible to total biomass, is a standing crop-selection criterion in space life support precisely because the inedible fraction isn't a rounding error. Add paper, wipes, food scraps and the solids the water plant deliberately won't touch, and a settlement accumulates a daily pile of carbon-rich material with nowhere to go.

On a station the answer is to dry it, bag it, and let it burn up with a departing cargo vehicle. At six people with resupply every few months that's a reasonable engineering call. At a settlement it isn't a call at all — it's a leak. Every kilogram of carbon and nitrogen that leaves is a kilogram somebody has to launch again, and the entire argument for building anything in this catalogue is that launching things again is the thing to stop doing.

So the solids have to be opened up and put back. The only question is what does the opening.

## Why not a digester

The conventional answer is anaerobic bacterial digestion. Faster, industrially mature on every continent, and it makes methane, which is both a fuel and a feedstock. MY-1 turns it down for four reasons, and the fourth is the real one.

**Lignin has nothing to recognise.** The inedible fraction of a crop is mostly lignocellulose, and lignin is the part that resists. It isn't a regular polymer — it's a randomised three-dimensional network of phenylpropanoid units joined by a mix of carbon–carbon and ether bonds in no fixed order. There's no repeating bond for a specific enzyme to bind and cleave; you can't shape an active site to fit a shape that's different every time. White-rot fungi solve this by giving up on specificity entirely: they secrete oxidative enzymes *outside* the cell — lignin peroxidase, manganese peroxidase, laccase — which generate free radicals and let unguided radical chemistry tear the network apart. Kirk and Farrell called it "enzymatic combustion," and the phrase is literal rather than poetic. It's a slow, enzyme-lit burn. Bacteria have no equivalent at useful scale, which is why a digester fed crop residue hands back the cellulose and leaves the lignin sitting in the tank.

**Indiscriminate is a feature here.** Because the attack is radical-mediated and unselective, the same system degrades a broad range of compounds it never evolved to meet — the long-standing biotech interest in *Pleurotus* for industrial contaminants and xenobiotics. Concept 49's most uncomfortable admission was that a closed loop concentrates whatever nobody thought to test for. A deliberately unselective oxidiser sitting in the solids stream is a partial answer to that.

**It runs in air.** No flammable gas accumulating in a sealed pressurised volume, and no sour-crash failure mode where the acid-formers outrun the methane-formers, the pH collapses, and the culture takes weeks to come back.

**And it can be looked at.** This is the design criterion, and it's about people rather than throughput. A digester is a closed vessel whose health is *inferred* — gas composition, pH, volatile fatty acid ratios — by somebody who knows how to read those numbers and is paying attention that week. A mycelial block is legible on sight. Healthy colonisation is an even white mat advancing at a rate anyone can judge. *Trichoderma* contamination is green and unmistakable. A stalled block announces itself by colour and smell to a person with no training whatsoever.

In a settlement where the maintenance crew is whoever's free, a process that reports its own condition in a form the human eye already reads is worth a large penalty in speed. MY-1 is slower than a digester on purpose. Forty days a block, against a digester's days.

And the output is food. *Pleurotus ostreatus* isn't exotic — it's among the most widely cultivated edible mushrooms in the world, grown commercially on agricultural residue. One organism turns waste lignocellulose into edible protein with no intermediate step and no second culture. Nothing bacterial does that.

## Two trains and a gap

**Train A — food.** Crop residue, paper, cardboard, cotton. Chopped, wetted, and *pasteurised* rather than sterilised — a few hours near 65 °C, which leaves a competing microflora in place that resists invasion better than a sterile substrate does. Bagged, inoculated in a clean anteroom, racked in the dark for eighteen days, then shocked into fruiting and harvested.

**Train B — mineral.** Human solids and anything that touched them. Thermally *sterilised* first, and that step is not negotiable. Separate fungal bed, separate tools, separate gloves, separate people on separate shifts. Output never eaten, and it only enters the growing medium after a second thermal pass.

**The air gap.** The most important feature of this design is the absence of something. There's no pipe, duct or fitting between Train A and Train B, and the drawing shows the separation as a physical gap rather than a valve — because a valve can be opened and a gap can't. They share a building, a power bus and a control system. They share nothing that carries matter or air.

Everything sits inside a shell held below habitat pressure, exhausting through HEPA, entered through a gowning anteroom. Two separate exhaust stacks, because a shared one would be a shared air handler and that would quietly undo the whole point.

**The gate.** At day eighteen, a block that isn't evenly colonised is pulled and incinerated. Not rescued, not re-inoculated, not set aside to see how it develops. The cost of a contaminated block entering the fruiting room is the cost of the whole room.

## The part that should worry you

This design cultivates a fungus inside the only atmosphere the settlement has.

That sentence is the concept's central liability and no containment engineering makes it untrue. Fungal and microbial contamination of crewed spacecraft isn't hypothetical — environmental surveys aboard the ISS documented a persistent and varied biological load on interior surfaces and in the air, and that was a vehicle where nobody was growing fungi on purpose. Negative pressure, HEPA and gowning reduce the hazard; they don't remove it. A containment failure in a sealed habitat is a different category of event from the same failure in a terrestrial mushroom farm that has a door to the outdoors.

A settlement that reviews this concept and decides the risk is unacceptable has made a defensible decision, and this study doesn't argue with it.

Two more honest costs. It wants floor area, and pressurised floor area is the scarcest commodity a habitat has — a digester occupies a tank, this occupies a room. And white rot *mineralises* carbon: solid carbon becomes CO₂. The vat moves carbon out of the solids loop and into the gas loop, where the algal loop and the Sabatier plant then have to absorb it. It's a node, not a sink, and a settlement that installs it without sizing the gas side has simply moved the problem.

## The thing both machines are about

Concepts 49 and 50 are the same argument made twice. Both are built from chemistry and biology that wouldn't surprise a competent terrestrial engineer. Both are limited not by what the machine can do but by what people will accept from it. Lethe asks a crew to drink. Saprophyte asks them to eat, and to keep a fungus alive in their air while they do.

The answer in both cases is legibility — but they reach for it differently, and the difference is the point. Lethe has to *buy* legibility with instruments, a ledger and a carafe on a sightline, because distillation is invisible and has to be translated. The vat doesn't need translating. A white mat on a rack is already a readable statement about the health of a living process, and anyone can read it without being taught.

That's why the slower organism won. In a place where everyone is responsible for everything, a machine that explains itself to a tired person at a glance is worth more than a machine that's merely faster.
